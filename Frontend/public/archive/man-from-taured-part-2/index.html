CHAPTER 7: THE FIRST BROADCAST

Rick stood in the monastery's small broadcasting room, the ancient stone walls lined with modern equipment that looked oddly out of place against the medieval architecture. The camera's red light blinked, waiting. He had spent three days preparing for this moment—writing, rewriting, and finally discarding his notes entirely.

"Three hours," Bartholomew said quietly from the doorway. "That's how long your testimony will run if you say everything you need to say."

Rick nodded. Three hours to explain an entire world. Three hours to tell people that everything they knew was a carefully constructed lie.

"The Southern Alliance is ready to distribute it," John added. "We've tapped into twenty-three independent networks across Europe, Asia, and the Americas. Once it's out, it can't be pulled back."

"Good." Rick adjusted the microphone clipped to his collar. "Because once I start talking, there's no going back."

The recording began simply. Rick looked into the camera and introduced himself: Rick Taur, formerly of Taured, a world that existed in a dimension parallel to this one. A world where humanity had won the war against the DRUJ corporation, where the Vimana airships still flew, where augmentation was voluntary and reversible.

Then he told them what this world had become.

He spoke about the neural implants that millions wore, believing them to be tools for optimization and connection. He explained how the implants worked—not just recording data and regulating mood, but subtly altering perception, suppressing doubt, redirecting thought away from uncomfortable truths.

"You're not broken," Rick said to the camera, his voice steady. "You're being broken. Deliberately. Systematically. The people who designed these systems studied human psychology for decades. They know exactly how to make you doubt your own instincts while trusting theirs."

He detailed the economic systems that kept people dependent—the subscription models for everything from housing to healthcare, the algorithmic pricing that squeezed every last credit from those who could least afford it, the way debt was weaponized to prevent organizing and resistance.

"In my world," Rick continued, "we asked ourselves a simple question: What if the goal isn't to help people but to control them? What if the entire system—the implants, the algorithms, the economic pressure—what if it's all designed to create a population that's too tired, too distracted, and too medicated to fight back?"

He paused, letting that sink in.

"And then we asked: What would resistance look like?"

The second hour of the testimony focused on practical steps. Rick explained the signs that someone's augmentation was degrading their autonomy—the inability to remember simple personal details, the compulsive touching of implant ports, the way anger or doubt seemed to dissolve before fully forming.

He talked about how to test yourself, how to trust your gut when something felt wrong even if your enhanced cognitive functions insisted everything was fine.

"The implants don't make you smarter," Rick said. "They make you compliant. There's a difference."

The final hour was the hardest. Rick spoke directly to those who worked for DRUJ and its subsidiaries—the engineers, the managers, the security personnel. He told them he understood their position. They had families to feed, careers to protect. The system rewarded loyalty and punished dissent.

"But you know," Rick said, his voice dropping to something almost gentle. "Deep down, you know. You've seen the data you're not supposed to see. You've written code you felt uncomfortable writing. You've watched people change after implantation and told yourself it's all voluntary, all beneficial."

He leaned forward slightly.

"I'm not asking you to blow up buildings or sabotage systems. I'm asking you to do something much harder: Remember who you were before. Remember what you believed. And when the time comes—and it will come—I'm asking you to choose differently. To leak a document. To delay an order. To look the other way when someone escapes. To do anything, however small, that puts humanity before the algorithm."

When the recording finally ended, Rick sat in silence for a long moment. The camera's red light blinked off. In the control room, Bartholomew and the others were motionless, processing what they'd just witnessed.

"That's going to start something," Matthew finally said.

"That's the idea," Rick replied.

THE AFTERMATH: THE AWAKENING

The video went viral within six hours of release. The title was simple: "The Awakening: A Testimony from Another World."

At first, the response was predictable. DRUJ's media subsidiaries immediately labeled it an elaborate hoax—impressive special effects, they said, likely funded by one of the anti-augmentation terrorist groups. Fact-checkers working for tech platforms flagged it as misinformation. Social media algorithms attempted to throttle its spread.

But the attempts at suppression only amplified interest. People shared it through encrypted channels, downloaded copies, and reposted it faster than it could be removed. Within twenty-four hours, it had been viewed over one hundred million times.

The comments sections exploded. Some dismissed Rick as a delusional conspiracy theorist. Others—many others—said his words resonated with feelings they'd been unable to articulate. Augmented citizens posted testimonies about strange gaps in their memory, about reaching for their implant ports during moments of doubt, about the unsettling sensation that their thoughts weren't entirely their own.

Support groups formed overnight. People began creating simple tests to check their own cognitive independence—questions designed to provoke emotional responses that the implants would try to suppress.

And then the messages started coming.

The Southern Alliance received 3,847 contacts in the first week. Some were from individuals wanting to join the resistance. Others were from engineers and scientists working within DRUJ's systems, offering to share information. A few were from government officials in smaller nations, asking for diplomatic contact.

One message stood out above all the others. It came from Elena Santos, a former Portuguese military officer who had been building a resistance network across southern Europe for three years. Her message was brief:

"We've been waiting for someone like you. We're ready to act. Tell us how to help."

Rick read the message three times, feeling something shift in his chest. He wasn't alone in this fight anymore. The scattered pieces of resistance were finding each other, forming a network.

It was the beginning of something larger than himself. And while he couldn't return home yet, couldn't see Christina and the children, he could at least be useful here. He could honor the life he'd been given in this strange dimension by fighting to free it.

"Send her our coordinates," Rick told Bartholomew. "Tell her we're ready to meet."

Outside the monastery window, the first snow of winter began to fall. Rick watched it drift down, thinking about his family on the other side of the dimensional barrier. He thought about the life he'd left behind and the life he was building here.

The work had just begun.

CHAPTER 8: THE SOUTHERN ALLIANCE

The transport helicopter touched down in a field outside Évora just after dawn. Rick, still unaccustomed to the way this dimension's aircraft vibrated differently from Vimana airships, gripped his seat during the descent. The Ark remained cloaked two kilometers away, monitoring the meeting site for threats.

Elena Santos waited beside a dusty van, arms crossed, watching the helicopter with an expression Rick couldn't quite read. Mid-forties, he guessed, with gray streaking her tied-back hair and the kind of stance that suggested military training. Two others stood with her—a younger man with a laptop bag slung over his shoulder and an older woman wearing priest's robes.

Rick climbed out, and Elena's eyes locked onto the bioluminescent patterns on his forearms before meeting his gaze.

"So it's real," she said. Not a question.

"It's real."

"Good." She extended a hand. "Because if you'd been some elaborate hoax, I was going to shoot you for wasting my time."

Rick shook her hand, noting the calluses and the firm grip. "Fair."

"This is Marco"—she gestured to the young man—"our tech specialist. And Sister Beatriz, who handles logistics and keeps us from descending into complete chaos."

The priest smiled. "Someone has to maintain order when we're planning revolution."

Elena led them to the van, and they drove into the countryside as the sun climbed higher. The landscape here reminded Rick painfully of the hills around Taured—the same golden-brown grass, the same ancient olive trees clinging to rocky soil. He looked away.

"Your broadcast reached us three days after you released it," Elena said, navigating a narrow road between stone walls. "We've been organizing resistance cells for three years, but we've been isolated. Small acts of sabotage, helping people escape augmentation facilities, documenting DRUJ's operations. Nothing coordinated. Until you."

"How many cells?" Rick asked.

"Forty-three active groups across Portugal, Spain, and southern France. Another dozen in North Africa. We stay small, compartmentalized. Safer that way." She glanced at him in the rearview mirror. "But your testimony changed the calculation. We can't stay small forever. Eventually, we have to offer people an alternative."

They arrived at a compound—an old monastery, Rick realized with irony, converted into what looked like a self-sufficient farm. Solar panels covered the southern-facing roofs. Greenhouses dotted the grounds. In the central courtyard, perhaps two hundred people had gathered, sitting on benches or standing in small groups. They quieted as Elena's van pulled through the gates.

"My core council," Elena explained. "Forty-three representatives, one from each cell. They've been waiting to meet you."

Rick stepped out into the courtyard, and the weight of their attention was almost physical. These weren't soldiers—most of them looked like ordinary people. Teachers, farmers, engineers, parents. But their eyes held something fierce.

Elena made brief introductions—names Rick knew he'd never remember all at once—and then gestured to a makeshift stage at one end of the courtyard.

"We need to know," she said simply. "Can they be saved? The augmented ones. Because if we're fighting to free people who can't be freed, we need to know that now."

Rick climbed onto the stage and looked out at the assembled faces. He thought about Morrison's hand touching her implant port. About Beaumont unable to remember what he'd loved. About his own cognitive functions detecting the subtle wrongness in their responses.

"Yes," he said. "They can be saved. The implants can be removed."

A murmur ran through the crowd.

"In my dimension, we developed the extraction procedure after the war. It's not simple—the neural interfaces integrate deep into the brain's architecture. But it can be done safely with the right equipment and training. The person's cognitive functions return. Their memories remain intact. They become themselves again."

"You're sure?" a man near the front asked.

"I've seen it work hundreds of times."

"Then teach us," Elena said.

THE RESISTANCE TAKES SHAPE

The meeting lasted six hours. Rick explained everything he knew about the augmentation technology—the neural architecture, the suppression algorithms, the way the implants tracked and predicted behavior. Marco took frantic notes, occasionally asking technical questions that Rick struggled to answer without his dimension's terminology.

Sister Beatriz asked the practical questions: How long did extraction take? What supplies were needed? Could it be done in a field hospital, or did it require a full medical facility?

But it was an older man—a former engineer named Paulo—who asked the hardest question.

"My son has an implant," he said quietly. "He stopped calling me two years ago. Says I'm holding him back, that my unaugmented thinking is limiting. When I see him now, it's like talking to someone wearing his face." His voice cracked. "If we remove the implant, will I get my son back?"

Rick met his eyes. "I don't know. The implant doesn't erase who people are—it suppresses certain responses and enhances others. Your son is still in there. But whether he'll want the same relationship with you afterward... that's his choice to make, not the implant's. I can only promise you'll be talking to him, not to DRUJ's algorithm."

Paulo nodded slowly, wiping his eyes.

As the sun began to set, Elena called for a decision.

"We can keep hiding," she said to the assembled group. "Keep our cells small and safe. Or we can do what Rick is suggesting—build something larger. A network that offers extraction, shelter, and a path forward for those who want to leave the system."

"They'll come after us," someone said.

"They're already coming after us," Elena replied. "The question is whether we want to still be running when they arrive, or whether we want to be ready."

A woman in her sixties stood. "I vote we build it."

Another voice: "I agree."

And then another, and another, until forty-three voices had risen to support the plan.

Elena looked at Rick. "Looks like you're staying awhile."

"I need to coordinate with the Ark," Rick said. "But yes. I'll stay as long as you need me."

That night, as the compound settled into quiet, Rick stood on the monastery's wall and watched the stars. The constellations were the same as home—different dimensional versions of the same universe—and somehow that made the distance feel even more vast.

His comm device beeped softly. A message from the Ark:

"Commander Chen has reported no contact for 48 hours. Search expanding. Your family is being notified of your status: Missing, presumed dimensional displacement. I am sorry, Rick."

Rick read it three times, each word a fresh wound. Christina would know by now. She'd be standing in their home in Taured, trying to explain to their children why Daddy wasn't coming home. Jayden would be crying. Ellie would be calculating probabilities. Asha wouldn't understand at all.

"I'm sorry," Rick whispered to the stars. "I'm so sorry."

But he couldn't go back. Not yet. Not while this dimension's humanity was enslaved. The cosmic forces that had brought him here seemed to have a purpose, and until he fulfilled it, the barrier between worlds would remain impassable.

He would finish the work. He would free this world.

And then—maybe then—he could find his way home.

CHAPTER 9: THE SEARCH BACK HOME

Taured Operations Center Tuesday, 0600 Hours

Commander Sarah Chen stared at the display showing Ark Seventeen's last known position. The holographic map floated above her workstation, a glowing path that traced the craft's flight from Taured to Forward Base Epsilon in what should have been a routine supply run.

The path ended abruptly in the desert. No distress beacon. No wreckage. No trace.

"Forty-eight hours," Lieutenant Marcus Webb said quietly beside her. He'd been Rick's co-pilot before his promotion. "How does an Ark vanish for forty-eight hours without leaving any trace?"

Sarah pulled up the scanning data again, knowing she wouldn't find anything new. The Vimana airships were designed to be nearly indestructible—living wood and crystal, powered by principles that still weren't fully understood. If one had crashed, there would be debris. Radiation signatures. Something.

"Show me the weather patterns," Sarah said.

Marcus overlaid meteorological data. Clear skies. Minimal wind. No storms, no unusual electromagnetic activity. Nothing that would explain an Ark simply disappearing.

"Could they have been shot down?" Marcus asked.

"By who? DRUJ hasn't had functioning weapons in this sector for three years. And even if they did, the Ark would have transmitted a distress call before impact."

Sarah drummed her fingers on the console, mind racing through scenarios. Mechanical failure was possible but unlikely. Arks didn't just break—they were semi-sentient, capable of self-repair. Pilot error? Rick Taur was one of their most experienced commanders. Navigation malfunction? The Ark would have compensated.

That left only impossible options.

"Pull the classified files," Sarah said. "Theoretical research. Dimensional studies."

Marcus hesitated. "Commander, those are sealed—"

"Pull them."

He complied, accessing files that most pilots didn't know existed. Research into Vimana propulsion had led to some unexpected discoveries about the nature of space-time. The Arks didn't just fly through normal space—they operated on principles that suggested multiple dimensions, parallel realities overlapping.

Sarah read through the theoretical papers, her unease growing. One paper, written by a physicist named Dr. Elena Reyes, proposed that under certain conditions, a Vimana craft could experience what she called "dimensional displacement"—slipping from one reality into a parallel version.

"Conditions," Sarah muttered. "What conditions?"

"Extreme emotional states in the pilot," Marcus read over her shoulder. "Combined with specific harmonic frequencies in the ship's crystal matrix. The paper suggests that a Vimana is partially consciousness-driven, responding to the pilot's mental state."

Sarah thought about Rick. She'd known him for five years, served with him during the final operations against DRUJ. He was steady, professional, good under pressure. But lately... lately she'd noticed something. A distance in his eyes. Like part of him was somewhere else.

"His wife," Sarah said suddenly.

"What?"

"Christina. They've been having problems. Rick told me a month ago. Nothing major, just... the stress of his deployment schedule. He said he felt guilty about being away so much."

Marcus looked skeptical. "You think guilt made him slip into another dimension?"

"I think if anyone could accidentally wish themselves into a parallel reality while flying a semi-sentient magical airship, it would be Rick Taur." Sarah closed the files. "But that's not something I can put in an official report."

"So what do we do?"

Sarah stood, straightening her uniform. "We do our jobs. Expand the search radius. Check every forward base, every settlement, every possible flight path. And I need to notify his family."

"Commander—"

"I know." Sarah met his eyes. "Believe me, I know."

TELLING CHRISTINA

Sarah drove to the Taur home herself. It was late afternoon when she arrived, the house sitting in one of Taured's older neighborhoods where trees lined the streets and children played in yards that actually had grass.

Christina answered the door with Asha on her hip, the two-year-old clutching a stuffed elephant. "Commander Chen." Christina's smile faded when she saw Sarah's expression. "What's wrong?"

"Can we talk inside?"

Christina knew before Sarah said anything—Sarah could see it in the way her face went still, the way she clutched Asha a little tighter. They sat in the living room while the children played in the next room, and Sarah explained as gently as she could.

"Forty-eight hours," Christina repeated, her voice flat. "He's been missing for two days and you're just telling me now?"

"We hoped we'd find him quickly. We didn't want to worry you unless—"

"Unless you couldn't find him at all." Christina set Asha down, the little girl toddling off to find her siblings. "Where is he, Sarah?"

"We don't know. We're expanding the search, checking with all allied forces. If anyone has seen him—"

"You think he's dead."

"I think the Ark is advanced enough that if Rick was in immediate danger, we'd have some indication. No news is actually good news in this case."

Christina laughed bitterly. "That's shit and you know it."

Sarah couldn't argue. She'd given that same empty reassurance to too many families over the years, and it had been a lie then too.

"I want to see the search data," Christina said.

"Mrs. Taur—"

"Commander, my husband is missing. Either you share the full report with me, or I start calling every journalist and council member I know until someone forces you to." Christina's voice was steel. "Your choice."

Sarah pulled out her tablet and shared the files. She watched Christina's eyes track across the data, her training as a logistics coordinator making the technical information accessible. Christina had worked in forward operations during the war—she knew how to read tactical reports.

"Dimensional displacement," Christina read aloud. "You think he slipped into another reality."

"It's one theory."

"Can you get him back?"

"We don't know how he left. Without understanding the mechanism—"

"So that's no." Christina set down the tablet. "You're telling me my husband is trapped in another dimension and you have no idea how to bring him back."

"We're exploring all options—"

"Get out."

Sarah stood. "Christina—"

"Get out of my house." Christina's voice broke. "Go search for him. Go pull every scientist you have off whatever they're working on and find my husband. But get out of my house."

Sarah left, hearing the door slam behind her. She stood on the porch for a long moment, then walked back to her vehicle. Through the window, she could see Christina collapsed on the couch, face in her hands.

Inside, the children gathered around their mother.

THE CHILDREN LEARN

Jayden, the oldest at nine, understood immediately. "Dad's not coming home, is he?"

Christina pulled herself together, wiping her eyes and gathering all three children close. "Daddy's missing. The Ark didn't come back from his mission."

"Missing isn't dead," Ellie said, her seven-year-old mind already calculating. "Missing means they don't know where he is. That's different."

"Where is he?" Asha asked, not understanding the weight of the word.

Christina tried to explain in terms a two-year-old could grasp. Daddy was lost. But the Ark was magic, and magic kept him safe. Everyone was looking for him.

Jayden cried. He pressed his face into Christina's shoulder and sobbed, and Christina held him while her own tears fell into his hair.

Ellie didn't cry. She just stared at nothing, her mind visibly working through scenarios and probabilities.

Asha, seeing her brother cry, started crying too, even though she didn't understand why.

"We're going to pray for him," Christina finally said. "We're going to pray every night that he's safe, that he's okay, and that he'll find his way home."

They knelt together in the living room, hands clasped. Christina had never been particularly religious, but Rick had taught the children to pray to whatever forces governed the universe. Good forces. Forces of light and love.

"Please," Christina whispered, "bring him home. Please."

In another dimension, Rick couldn't hear them. But somewhere in the quantum foam between realities, their love reached toward him like a thread in the dark.

THE SEARCH CONTINUES

Commander Chen's team expanded their search into DRUJ territory—areas where Taured forces rarely ventured. They scanned ruins, checked with resistance cells, interrogated prisoners. Nothing.

Dr. Reyes, the physicist who'd written about dimensional displacement, was pulled from her teaching position and brought into a classified facility. She worked day and night, trying to understand how to detect dimensional breaches and potentially create one deliberately.

"It's possible," she told Sarah after a week of research. "Theoretically. But it would require energy on a scale we've never attempted. And even if we could open a portal, how would we know which dimension he's in? There could be infinite parallel realities."

"So we just give up?"

"I'm not saying that." Dr. Reyes pulled up a complex diagram. "I'm saying we need a beacon. Something unique to Rick that we could track across dimensional boundaries. A specific harmonic signature."

"Like what?"

"Like another Ark. If we could get Ark Eighteen—Rick's backup craft—to resonate at the exact frequency of Ark Seventeen, it might create a kind of dimensional echo. We'd be able to sense where they are in relation to each other."

Sarah authorized the experiment. They brought Ark Eighteen into the lab, coaxing it to sing at the frequency of its sibling craft. The crystal matrix hummed, harmonics building.

And for just a moment—less than a second—Dr. Reyes's instruments detected something. A response. A whisper of presence from somewhere else.

"He's alive," Dr. Reyes breathed. "He's alive and the Ark is intact."

"Where?"

"Not where. When. Or... dimension. I'm not sure how to describe it." She showed Sarah the data. "But he's out there. And he's calling back."

Sarah stared at the readings. Somewhere, in a dimension so close yet impossibly distant, Rick Taur was still flying.

"Keep trying," Sarah ordered. "Every day. If there's a way to reach him, we're going to find it."

In the Taur home, Christina felt something shift. Like a distant bell ringing. She couldn't explain it, but she knew—absolutely knew—that Rick was alive.

She gathered the children and told them.

"Daddy's okay," she said. "Wherever he is, he's okay. And he's trying to come home."

CHAPTER 10: FIRST CONTACT WITH THE ENEMY

The alarm jolted Rick awake at 0400 hours. The Ark's warning system wasn't loud, but it vibrated through the living wood of the ship's hull in a way that made instant alertness unavoidable.

"Ark, report," Rick said, already pulling on clothes in the dark.

"Incoming military aircraft," the ship replied, its voice calm despite the urgency. "Three transport helicopters, estimated twelve minutes to arrival. Enhanced thermal signatures suggest augmented personnel."

Rick's blood went cold. They'd found him. More accurately, they'd found the Southern Alliance compound.

He reached the monastery's courtyard to find it already alive with activity. Elena was shouting orders, coordinating evacuation routes. Marco worked frantically at a portable terminal, wiping hard drives. Sister Beatriz was shepherding the compound's thirty-seven residents—most former DRUJ employees who'd come seeking extraction—toward hidden vehicles.

"Twelve minutes isn't enough," Elena said when she saw Rick. "We've got people still asleep, children—"

"I can buy you time."

"How?"

Rick gestured to the Ark, still cloaked at the edge of the compound's grounds. "Ever seen a fire that isn't real?"

He ran to the ship, reaching its interior in seconds. "Ark, I need the holographic projection system. Full power. Can you simulate fire?"

"I can simulate anything within my energy range."

"Good. When those helicopters arrive, I want them to see this compound burning. Make it look like a raid already happened, like they're too late. Evacuate everyone before they realize it's an illusion."

"An elegant deception." The Ark's crystal core pulsed approval. "Preparing simulation."

Rick watched through the ship's sensors as the Southern Alliance executed their emergency protocol with practiced efficiency. Vehicles moved out through concealed exits. People helped each other, nobody panicking, everyone knowing their role. This wasn't their first evacuation.

The helicopters appeared on the horizon at 0412 hours. Military transport, augmented pilots showing heat signatures far brighter than baseline humans. As they approached, the Ark activated its holographic system.

To anyone watching from the air, the compound erupted in flames. Fire rolled across rooftops, smoke billowed from windows. Bodies lay scattered in the courtyard—convincing illusions of casualties.

The helicopters circled once, then descended into the square. Rick watched a dozen soldiers disembark, weapons ready, scanning for survivors. In the lead was someone different—a woman in civilian clothes, no combat gear, but moving with the command presence that marked an officer.

"That's Morrison," Rick breathed. Director Sarah Morrison, one of DRUJ's senior security officials. The Ark had downloaded her profile from intercepted communications.

Morrison walked through the illusory flames, her augmented vision clearly detecting something wrong but not quite placing what. She touched her implant port—Rick watched her hand move to the subtle interface behind her ear—and her expression flickered.

"Something's off," she said to her second-in-command. "This fire doesn't feel right."

Rick made a decision. "Ark, drop the illusion. And make sure I look impressive."

The holographic fire vanished. The compound stood intact, empty except for the military personnel now realizing they'd been deceived. And the Ark—thirty meters of living wood and crystal, its hull covered in bioluminescent patterns that cast the courtyard in shifting blue-green light—materialized above the courtyard.

Rick descended the landing ramp, letting the ship's internal illumination silhouette him against the light. The Ark's hum filled the air, a sound that vibrated in bone and teeth.

Morrison's soldiers raised weapons, but she held up a hand. She stared at Rick, at the Ark, her tactical mind clearly trying to process what she was seeing.

"Rick Taur," Morrison said. "The man from the viral video."

"Director Morrison," Rick replied, stopping at the base of the ramp. His hands were visible, empty. "You're a long way from Barcelona."

"You've been broadcasting some very interesting claims. Creating quite a bit of unrest." She walked closer, her augmented guards forming a protective circle. "That ship of yours... I've never seen technology like it."

"You wouldn't have. It's not from here."

Morrison's hand touched her implant port again. A nervous gesture, or was the implant suggesting she doubt her perceptions? Rick activated his cognitive functions, analyzing her micro-expressions, her breathing patterns.

"You're wondering if I'm telling the truth," Rick said. "About the parallel dimension. About what the implants really do." He took a step forward. Morrison's guards tensed, but she waved them off. "Ask yourself: When was the last time you felt angry? Truly angry? Not frustrated or annoyed, but genuinely furious?"

Morrison's expression flickered.

"Can't remember?" Rick asked gently. "When was the last time you cried? The last time you felt overwhelmed, or heartbroken, or so joyful you couldn't contain it?"

"Those responses are inefficient," Morrison said, but her voice lacked conviction. "They impair judgment."

"They're human. And you're not allowed to be human anymore." Rick gestured to his own head. "The implant in your skull is regulating your emotional responses right now. You're standing in front of an impossible ship, confronting someone who claims to be from another dimension, and you feel calm. Analytical. Like you're reading a report."

Her hand moved to her implant port again.

"That right there," Rick said. "You keep touching it. Do you know why?"

Morrison's eyes narrowed. "Explain."

"Because some part of you—the part the implant hasn't suppressed yet—knows something is wrong. It's trying to signal you. And the implant is responding by increasing its regulatory function, which creates a feedback loop. Your brain sends stress signals, the implant calms them, which causes more stress because your instincts know you should be stressed, and around it goes."

"That's an interesting theory."

"It's not a theory." Rick looked directly into her eyes. "What's your first name?"

The question visibly caught her off guard. "Director—"

"Not your title. Your name. The one your parents gave you."

Morrison opened her mouth, then paused. For just a second, Rick saw confusion cross her face. "Sarah," she finally said.

"You had to think about it." Rick's voice was gentle. "Your own name. Something you should know as instinctively as breathing. But the implant has reordered your identity priorities. Your role is primary. Your self is secondary."

Morrison's hand went to her implant port again, and this time Rick saw something in her expression shift. Fear? Recognition?

"You're under arrest," she said, but it sounded more like a question.

"I'm offering myself," Rick replied. "Voluntarily. No resistance. I'll go with you, submit to whatever examination you want, and answer every question truthfully. On one condition."

"You're not in a position to make conditions."

"Neither are you." Rick gestured around the empty compound. "Everyone you wanted to capture is gone. You have no tactical advantage here. The only thing you're leaving with is me—if I choose to let you take me."

Morrison studied him for a long moment. "What's the condition?"

"Ask yourself—really ask yourself—if you're making this decision, or if the implant is making it for you."

He watched her face, saw the internal war playing out. Her hand touched the implant port again, and this time she seemed to notice the gesture, her expression shifting to something like horror.

"Detain him," she finally ordered. "Full security protocols."

Rick raised his hands as soldiers approached. As they placed restraints on his wrists, he caught Morrison's eyes one last time.

"I'll see you in Barcelona," he said. "And Sarah? Start paying attention to your hands."

EXTRACTION

They loaded Rick into the lead helicopter as the Ark watched from above, cloaked but tracking. Bartholomew's voice came through Rick's concealed comm device.

"This is a terrible plan."

"It's the only plan," Rick murmured, quiet enough that the helicopter's noise covered his words. "Morrison is cracking. I saw it. Her implant is losing its hold. If I can get inside their facility, document their operations from the inside..."

"They're going to dissect you."

"They're going to try to understand me. There's a difference." Rick settled into his seat, flanked by two augmented guards whose expressions showed the flat affect he was starting to recognize. "Stay in range. When I need an exit, I'll signal."

"And if you can't?"

"Then you bring the whole building down."

The helicopter lifted off, banking toward Barcelona. Rick watched the monastery grounds shrink below, knowing he'd just traded his freedom for the slim possibility of reaching one woman's suppressed humanity.

The question was whether Morrison had enough humanity left to reach.

CHAPTER 11: INSIDE THE MACHINE

The interrogation room was white. Not just white walls—white floors, white ceiling, white furniture, all lit by panels that created a diffuse, shadowless illumination that made it impossible to judge distance or depth. It was disorienting, and Rick suspected that was the point.

He'd been sitting in the chair—restraints removed but a electromagnetic field humming in the walls, preventing the Ark from communicating with him directly—for three hours when Morrison finally entered.

She looked different than she had in the field. Here, in her domain, she carried herself with absolute authority. But Rick noticed her hand go to her implant port twice in the first minute.

"Comfortable?" she asked, taking the seat across from him.

"I've been in worse places."

"I doubt that." Morrison placed a tablet on the table between them. "This is our initial assessment. Baseline human physiology with some interesting deviations. Your cellular structure shows markers we've never seen before. Your neural pathways are developing in ways that suggest... well, my science team is arguing about whether you're enhanced or just unusual."

"I'm normal," Rick said. "For my dimension."

Morrison's expression didn't change, but her hand twitched toward her implant. "Let's talk about that. Your dimension. You claim to come from a parallel reality where history diverged approximately forty years ago."

"Not quite. History diverged much earlier. Forty years ago is when the divergence became obvious—when your world chose augmentation and mine didn't."

"Because you won a war we lost."

"Because humanity in my world recognized the threat in time." Rick leaned forward slightly. "Morrison—can I call you Sarah?"

Her eyes narrowed. "No."

"Director Morrison, then. You know what bothers me most about this world? It's not the augmentation itself. Technology for enhancing human capability isn't inherently evil. It's the lying. The pretending that the implants are tools when they're chains."

"They're liberation," Morrison said, but something in her voice wavered. "Freedom from depression, anxiety, cognitive limitation—"

"Freedom from doubt. That's what you mean, isn't it? Freedom from the uncomfortable feeling that something might be wrong." Rick watched her hand move to her implant again. "When was the last time you questioned an order?"

"I question orders constantly. That's my job."

"Tactical questioning. Strategic analysis. But when was the last time you wondered if the entire mission was wrong? If DRUJ itself might be the problem, not the solution?"

Morrison's hand was at her implant port now, fingers touching the subtle interface. "This is an interesting interrogation technique. Are you actually trying to convert me?"

"I'm trying to remind you that you're human."

The door opened, and a man entered—mid-fifties, expensive suit, the kind of polished appearance that suggested executive rather than military. He smiled warmly at Rick.

"Mr. Taur. I'm Dr. Laurent Beaumont, Chief Psychological Officer for the European Augmentation Initiative. I've been very interested to meet you."

Rick studied him. Another augmented officer, but unlike Morrison, Beaumont showed no signs of internal conflict. His expression was perfectly controlled, his movements precise. If the implant was suppressing doubt in him, it was doing a flawless job.

"Doctor," Rick acknowledged.

"I've been reviewing your testimony. It's quite fascinating—as a narrative, I mean. The level of detail, the internal consistency, the way you've constructed an entire alternate history." Beaumont pulled up a chair. "It's brilliant fiction."

"It's not fiction."

"Of course you believe that. The mind is incredibly good at constructing coherent realities. Do you know what we found most interesting in your neural scans? The belief centers of your brain show activation patterns consistent with absolute conviction. You genuinely believe you're from another dimension."

Rick felt a chill. "You scanned me?"

"While you were unconscious during transport. Standard procedure." Beaumont smiled apologetically. "You're not technically under arrest, but you are a security concern. We needed to understand what we're dealing with."

"And what did you find?"

"Anomalies. Fascinating ones." Beaumont pulled up imaging on his tablet, showing Rick's brain activity. "These patterns here—they're consistent with someone who has experienced genuine trauma-based memory formation. But there are no records of you anywhere. No childhood, no education history, no medical records. You appeared six months ago in this reality as if you'd always existed, but with no past."

"Because I came from somewhere else."

"Or," Beaumont said gently, "because you're suffering from a complex dissociative disorder combined with false memory syndrome. It's rare, but documented. The mind creates elaborate fiction to cope with trauma too painful to remember."

Rick looked at Morrison, who was watching this exchange with an expression he couldn't read. "You think I'm delusional."

"I think you're suffering," Beaumont replied. "And I want to help you."

"By making me accept that my memories are lies?"

"By helping you integrate back into a healthy relationship with reality. We have excellent treatment protocols. With proper augmentation, we could help you filter out the false memories, stabilize your identity, and give you a meaningful life in this world."

There it was. The offer Rick had been expecting since the moment they'd captured him. They weren't going to imprison him—they were going to fix him.

"What if I refuse?" Rick asked.

Beaumont's smile didn't waver. "That would be unfortunate. The alternative is to classify you as a persistent security threat, which means indefinite detention in a psychiatric facility. Not ideal for anyone."

Rick turned to Morrison. "And you agree with this assessment?"

She met his eyes, and for just a moment, Rick saw something flicker. Doubt? Recognition? Her hand touched her implant port.

"I think," Morrison said slowly, "that further evaluation is warranted."

"Evaluation," Rick repeated. "Not treatment."

"Not yet."

Beaumont looked between them, and something in his expression tightened. "Director Morrison, we've already authorized—"

"I'm authorizing further evaluation first," Morrison cut him off. Her voice was steel. "This man claims to have information about our augmentation protocols that suggests security vulnerabilities. Before we alter his cognitive state, I want to fully understand what he knows and how he knows it."

Beaumont's hand went to his own implant port—the same unconscious gesture—and Rick realized with a start that Morrison wasn't just deflecting. She was buying time. For herself, for him, or both.

"Very well," Beaumont said, standing. "But I'm putting this on record, Director. Every day we delay treatment is a day he could contaminate others with his delusions."

He left, and the room fell silent. Morrison stayed seated, staring at Rick.

"You're scared," Rick said quietly.

"I'm analytical."

"You're scared that I might be telling the truth. And you're scared of what it means if your implant has been suppressing that possibility." Rick leaned forward. "What did you love, Sarah? Before the implant. What made you happy?"

Her hand went to the port. She seemed to realize it this time, pulling her hand away like she'd touched something hot.

"I can't remember," she whispered.

"That's not age. That's not stress. That's the system you serve." Rick's voice was gentle. "You've been optimized. Refined. Made into the perfect officer. And the cost was everything that made you who you were."

Morrison stood abruptly. "This session is over."

"Sarah—"

"Director Morrison." She walked to the door, then paused. Without turning around, she said: "The neural scan. The one Beaumont mentioned. There's a copy in the shared drive under your prisoner ID. If you're who you claim to be, you'll find it interesting reading."

Then she was gone, and Rick was alone in the white room.

He sat back, processing. Morrison had just given him access to classified data. Whether she knew it or not, she'd just chosen a side.

Now he just had to survive long enough to exploit it.

CHAPTER 12: THE NEURAL SCAN

Rick didn't sleep that night. The holding cell they'd placed him in was comfortable—more like a hotel room than a prison—but he spent the hours staring at the ceiling, thinking about Morrison's last words.

The neural scan. What had they found that Morrison wanted him to see?

Morning came with a breakfast tray delivered by an expressionless guard. Rick ate mechanically, his mind elsewhere. Around 0900, Morrison returned.

"Doctor Beaumont will be conducting a full psychological evaluation this afternoon," she said without preamble. "You'll answer his questions truthfully and completely."

"Or?"

"Or you'll answer them under chemical enhancement, which I think we'd both prefer to avoid." She placed a tablet on the table. "This is the neural scan data. You have two hours to review it before Beaumont arrives."

Rick picked up the tablet, checking that it was properly unlocked. "Why are you doing this?"

Morrison's expression was unreadable. "Because if you're right, I need to know. And if you're wrong..." She paused. "I spent three hours last night trying to remember what I was like before my augmentation. I can recall events—my job, my career progression, my achievements. But I can't remember how I felt. Can't remember what made me laugh, or what kept me up at night. It's all there in my memory, but it's like reading a file about someone else."

"That's the suppression protocol working."

"Or it's normal aging and stress." But she didn't sound convinced.

"Sarah, what's your favorite color?"

The question clearly caught her off guard. She opened her mouth, then closed it. Opened it again. "I... I don't know."

"Everyone knows their favorite color."

"I used to know." Morrison's hand went to her implant port, then fell away. "Blue. I think it was blue."

"You think."

"I don't... it's not important. Mission-critical data is prioritized. Personal preferences are optimized out to improve cognitive efficiency."

"You mean erased."

Morrison met his eyes, and Rick saw something he hadn't before: fear. Raw, undisguised fear.

"Read the scan," she said. "I'll be back in two hours."

THE DATA

Rick opened the neural scan file and immediately understood why Morrison had wanted him to see it.

The scan had captured his brain activity during the transport, when he'd been sedated but not fully unconscious. The imagery showed normal human brain structures—nothing alien or enhanced. But the memory centers showed something remarkable.

His memories weren't just consistent. They were encoded with a depth and complexity that suggested genuine experience, not fabrication. The scan had captured neural pathways forming in real-time as he'd dreamed during the flight—and those pathways referenced locations, people, and events that didn't exist in this dimension's historical records.

More than that, the scan had captured something else. A kind of harmonic resonance between Rick's neural activity and... something external. The technicians had flagged it as an artifact, probably interference from the helicopter's electronics.

But Rick knew what it was. The Ark. Even sedated, even shielded, his connection to the ship had maintained itself at a quantum level. His consciousness and the Ark's were entangled in a way that transcended simple neural implants.

He read through the technician notes:

"Subject shows memory encoding consistent with genuine experience. However, referenced events do not match any historical records. Recommend further analysis to determine if this represents a sophisticated false memory syndrome or an unknown phenomenon."

And then, buried in a footnote:

"Preliminary analysis suggests subject's memories may reference events from an alternate historical timeline. Recommend consultation with theoretical physics division. See attached paper by Dr. Elena Reyes, 'Quantum Branching and Dimensional Divergence in Historical Outcomes.'"

Rick laughed aloud. They'd found evidence of his claims buried in their own data and hadn't even realized it.

The door opened. Rick expected Morrison, but instead found Dr. Beaumont, arriving two hours early.

"Mr. Taur. I see you've been reviewing your scan." Beaumont smiled warmly. "What do you think?"

Rick set down the tablet. "I think your technicians found exactly what I said they would. Evidence that I'm telling the truth."

"Evidence of consistent memory encoding, yes. But consistency isn't truth. The human brain is remarkably good at creating coherent fiction."

"What did you love?" Rick asked. "Before your augmentation. What made Laurent Beaumont happy?"

Beaumont's smile didn't waver. "I struggled with depression. Anxiety. The implant freed me from that. Now I experience optimal well-being, maximum cognitive function. I've become the best version of myself."

"But what did you love? Music? Art? A person?"

"Those attachments were inefficient. They created vulnerability."

"They created humanity." Rick stood, and four guards immediately tensed. "Doctor, can I show you something?"

"That depends what it is."

Rick raised his hands slowly, showing them empty. "Just a gesture. May I?"

Beaumont nodded, curious.

Rick pressed his hands together, then pulled them apart slowly, letting his enhanced neurology activate the bioluminescent patterns on his forearms. Soft blue-green light traced across his skin in geometric patterns.

Beaumont's eyes widened. "How—"

"These patterns are genetic. Natural. In my dimension, they emerged after humanity rejected neural augmentation and instead explored other methods of enhancement. Meditation, breathwork, direct neural training without invasive technology." The patterns pulsed gently. "We learned to access our full potential without surrendering autonomy."

"That's not possible. The research shows—"

"The research shows what DRUJ wanted it to show." Rick let the patterns fade. "Doctor, your implant is suppressing your doubt right now. I can see it in your micro-expressions. Some part of you knows I'm telling the truth, and the augmentation is working to suppress that recognition."

Beaumont's hand went to his implant port. For the first time, his smile faltered.

"I'm going to return to my cell now," Rick continued. "I'm going to wait while you and Director Morrison continue to process what you've seen. And eventually, you're going to have to make a choice—accept that your entire system is built on suppressing human consciousness, or keep lying to yourself until there's nothing left of you to lie to."

Beaumont stared at him for a long moment. Then, without a word, he turned and left.

Rick sat back down, exhausted. The Ark's voice whispered in his mind—contact reestablished now that the EM shielding had been temporarily lowered.

"The facility's power grid has a vulnerability. When you're ready, I can create a localized electromagnetic pulse that will disrupt their systems for approximately ninety seconds."

"Not yet," Rick murmured. "Morrison is close to breaking through. I need to give her time."

"And if she doesn't?"

"Then we do this the hard way."

THE BREAKTHROUGH

It happened at 0300 hours. Rick was jolted awake by the sound of his cell door opening. Morrison stood in the doorway, still in her uniform but looking like she hadn't slept. Her hand wasn't at her implant port—it was wrapped around it, fingers pressed against the interface like she was trying to tear it out.

"I can't remember his name," she said without preamble.

Rick sat up slowly. "Whose name?"

"My son. I have a son. I know I do—I can remember giving birth, I can remember his childhood, his school, his—" Her voice broke. "But I can't remember his name. I can see his face, I can remember loving him, but the name is just... gone."

"The implant is suppressing it."

"Why would it suppress my own son's name?"

"Because remembering it, really remembering it, would make you feel something the implant can't regulate. Love. Real, overwhelming, parent-child love." Rick stood carefully, hands visible. "Sarah, the implant isn't just optimizing your cognition. It's pruning away everything that might make you hesitate, might make you question, might make you human."

Morrison's fingers pressed harder against the port. "I want it out."

"I can't remove it here. I don't have the equipment."

"But you know how."

"In my dimension, yes. We developed the extraction protocol. It's not simple, but it can be done." Rick took a step toward her. "Sarah, if you want this out, I need you to help me leave. Get me back to the Southern Alliance. They have medical facilities where we can—"

Alarms erupted throughout the facility. Red light flooded the corridors beyond Rick's cell.

Morrison's military training snapped back into place. She grabbed Rick's arm. "What did you do?"

"Nothing." Rick activated his comm. "Ark?"

"I detected an unauthorized access to the facility's detention systems. Someone just remotely unlocked every cell in this wing."

Morrison swore. "Beaumont. He's initiating a containment breach to justify extreme measures." She pulled Rick toward the door. "We have maybe three minutes before security realizes you're not a threat and starts looking for who is."

They ran through corridors filling with confused prisoners and rushing guards. Morrison's access codes got them through two security checkpoints, but at the third, an augmented soldier blocked their path.

"Director Morrison, you're not cleared for prisoner transport—"

"Override authorization Morrison-Alpha-Seven—"

"Denied. You've been flagged for psych evaluation."

Morrison's expression went cold. "Beaumont."

The guard raised his weapon. "Ma'am, please step away from the prisoner."

Rick made a split-second decision. His enhanced reflexes let him move faster than a baseline human—not superhuman, just optimized. He disarmed the guard with a technique Christina had taught him years ago, using the man's own momentum against him.

"Sorry," Rick said to the unconscious guard.

Morrison stared. "How—"

"Later. Where's the exit?"

"Roof. Emergency helicopter." She was running again, and Rick followed. They burst through a maintenance door onto the facility's roof, where a helicopter sat waiting in the predawn darkness.

And found Beaumont already there, flanked by six augmented soldiers.

"Director Morrison," Beaumont said, his tone disappointed. "I had hoped you'd resist the contamination. But I see he's gotten to you too."

"Laurent, what have you done?"

"Initiated proper containment protocols. You've been compromised, Sarah. The prisoner has been feeding you delusions, exploiting your implant's degradation." He nodded to his soldiers. "Detain them both."

Morrison moved to fight, but Rick caught her arm. "Don't. We can't win."

"So we just surrender?"

"No." Rick looked up at the sky. "We get picked up."

The Ark materialized above the facility, its cloaking field dropping as it descended with impossible speed and silence. The soldiers opened fire, but their bullets sparked harmlessly off the living wood hull.

The ship's landing ramp extended, and Bartholomew appeared, grinning and holding what looked like a traditional arquebus modified with crystal technology. "Need a ride?"

Rick and Morrison ran for the ramp as the Ark's defensive systems created a barrier of light that knocked back the attacking soldiers. They dove into the ship's interior as the ramp retracted.

"Go!" Rick shouted.

The Ark surged upward, not away but directly through the facility's roof. The living wood hull, harder than diamond when it needed to be, smashed through concrete and steel like paper.

They burst into open air over Barcelona, the Ark leaving a perfect ship-shaped hole in the DRUJ facility's roof.

Morrison collapsed against the wall, staring at the impossible interior of an impossible ship. "What have I done?"

"You chose humanity over optimization," Rick said. "Now let's get that implant out before it kills you."

The Ark leveled off, flying north toward the Southern Alliance. Behind them, Director Beaumont stood on the ruined roof, watching them disappear into the dawn.

He touched his implant port, and his expression went perfectly calm.

"Log tactical failure. Request reinforcement protocols. Priority: Continental."

The hunt had just begun.

EPILOGUE: BETWEEN DIMENSIONS

Rick collapsed in exhaustion as the Ark flew north, Morrison secured in the medical bay while the ship's systems worked to stabilize her degrading implant until proper extraction could be performed. They'd downloaded all the facility's data—including Morrison's own files, which contained detailed information about DRUJ's European operations.

Bartholomew sat beside Rick in the observation room, both of them watching the sunrise paint the Mediterranean gold and pink.

"You know they're going to escalate now," Bartholomew said. "We just kidnapped one of their senior directors."

"We saved her. There's a difference."

"Not to them."

Rick didn't answer. He was thinking about Morrison's son, about the name she couldn't remember. About his own children, whose names he whispered every night like a prayer.

The Ark hummed around them, its presence a comfort. "Passenger Taur, I have successfully downloaded all facility data. The neural scan records have been preserved. Additionally, I have detected something unusual."

"What?"

"In the quantum noise between dimensions, I am receiving a signal. Faint, but persistent. It resonates with your neural signature."

Rick's heart stopped. "From where?"

"From your dimension. From Ark Seventeen's sister ship. They are searching for you."

Rick stood, moving to the main display. The Ark showed him the quantum readings—a thread of connection so thin it was almost invisible, but unmistakably there.

"Can you communicate back?"

"Not directly. But if I continue to respond to their signal, they may be able to triangulate our dimensional position. In time, they might be able to create a stable portal."

"How much time?"

"Unknown. Months. Years. Possibly longer."

Rick closed his eyes, feeling tears slip down his face. Christina was looking for him. Sarah Chen was looking for him. They hadn't given up.

"Keep responding," Rick said. "Every day. Whatever it takes."

"Acknowledged."

Rick looked out at this dimension's Earth, at the sunrise that looked exactly like home but wasn't. At the work still ahead of him—freeing Morrison, supporting the Southern Alliance, building a resistance that could actually challenge DRUJ's global control.

It would take time. Maybe years. But now he knew: He wasn't lost. He wasn't forgotten. Somewhere, across the dimensional barrier, his family was waiting.

And he would find his way back to them.

"I'm sorry," he whispered to the sky. "I'm trying. I promise I'm trying."

In another dimension, in a home in Taured, Christina woke from a dream of golden light. She reached out in the darkness, her hand falling on empty sheets where her husband should be.

But for the first time in weeks, she didn't cry. She knew, somehow, that he was alive. That he was fighting.

That he would come home.
